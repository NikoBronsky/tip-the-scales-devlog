# Dev log — Tip the Scales, an entry for the 2026 Epic AI MegaJam (team One Man Raft)

One human, many agents. The human is the game director, who decides what the game is, playtests and submits. AI agents do the production work: code, scene, effects, audio, builds, and the gameplay video, which the director watches and accepts. This log is written during the jam, entry by entry, so that the whole process is visible.

Theme: "Director's Cut". Our reading: a personal cut of an ordinary mechanic. We take fishing and make our own version of it.

Times are CET (UTC+1). The jam starts on 12 Nov 2026 at 06:00 and ends on 16 Nov 2026 at 02:59.

---

## Day 0 — before the start (11 Nov evening to 12 Nov 06:00)

No game code, no game assets and no levels were made before the start. This entry lists everything that was prepared, as the rules ask.

### Talking and planning
- The director and the orchestrator agent agreed on the direction: a small fishing game at sea, seen as a tilted side cut of the water, where upgrades bend the rules. One scene, polish over breadth.
- The agent drew throwaway browser sketches (HTML canvas) to compare camera projections and to see how the picture scales with many rods, hooks and fish. They were planning aids only. They are not in the repository and not in the game.

- After the theme reveal the orchestrator wrote a four-day plan with a day-one work order (`Docs/plan.md`, in Russian). A second agent (Opus) was asked to attack the plan. Its nine findings changed it: content must be approved by the director before it is built, the editor is restarted only in planned windows, two cheap ways of drawing the water are compared before one is chosen, the first build is already a full loop from title screen to restart.
- The partner perks email arrived during the night. The director decided not to claim extra perks for now. Agents do not create accounts or sign in.
- At about 01:00 the director answered the open plan questions by voice. The sea is a cut-out block, like a diorama. The shop is an interface layer between days, on the same screen. The player does not chase bites with reaction clicks: a rod is cast, the hook sinks to its depth, a fish bites and hangs on the hook, and the player pulls the rod out. A rod that pulls itself is an upgrade. A special fish changes one rule of its day. The run is endless and the daily fee keeps rising. No nets in the first version. The orchestrator writes the list of fish, baits and upgrades and shows it to the director.
- The director's main point: this game wins by feel, not by the amount of content. Every action must answer beautifully: the cast, the sinking hook, the bite, the pull, an item that follows the mouse. Few assets.
- The fish will be a simple low-poly model with a side texture, made by an agent in Blender after the start and animated by a shader, so that the sea can hold up to a thousand fish.
- At the director's request the orchestrator built a throwaway browser prototype of the fishing loop: one HTML page with a canvas, published as a private Claude artifact. It is a decision aid. The director can feel the rod loop in it and compare three variants of one open rule (what happens to a fish that hangs on the hook). The page is not in the repository. No code, art or sound from it goes into the Unreal game; the game is written from scratch after the start. Its fish, baits, upgrades and numbers are placeholders. A script played ten in-game days through it without errors before the director saw it.
- The director played the prototype and liked it. He listed what to change. Bait must come from packs that offer a choice of baits. There must be more baits, each labelled with the fish it catches. A run starts with one rod. The game needs modifiers in four classes (rod, bait, fish, sea) and a limit on them, so that players build different strategies. Pauses between fish must be shorter. The day starts when the player presses a button, after a calm preparation step. Putting bait on a rod needs a clearer gesture. Rods must stay visible when the view scrolls down. Depth opens step by step. Every action answers with motion and sound. A hooked fish can escape, and one modifier removes that.
- Before the start the orchestrator turned this feedback into a content list (`Docs/design/content-list.md`): a critique of four design choices, eight fish, nine baits, bait packs, three upgrades, twenty modifiers, three depth zones, the fee curve, the special fish and a table of feedback events. It is a plan on paper. All names and numbers are placeholders until a build is played.
- The orchestrator handed the second version of the browser prototype to an Opus sub-agent with a written brief (`Docs/briefs/prejam-prototype-v2.md`), so that the director can try these ideas before the start. The same rule holds: nothing from the prototype goes into the Unreal game.
- The director answered the content list by voice. Five depth zones, plus a seabed far below for bottom fishing. Baits get rarity tiers. A bare hook still catches a little, so the player never gets stuck. The shelf holds five modifiers, and some items may change that number. A combo is a chain: a bigger fish eats the hooked fish and gets hooked itself, then a third one may follow. The game must show clearly when a fish is about to escape, because in the prototype he could not tell whether to pull or to wait. He rejected the orchestrator's idea of a bait socket at each rod as clumsy. The sea must feel alive: schools, drifting floats, swaying plants, a turtle. The orchestrator wrote the second edition of the content list and sent the sub-agent a delta brief (`Docs/briefs/prejam-prototype-v2-delta.md`).
- The director added one more wish: at the end of the day the catch is shipped fish by fish, with a coin popping up for each one, so that the player enjoys every fish a second time. Fish are therefore sold at day end. The orchestrator checked that this changes no rule, because money is only spent in the shop between days, and added it to the content list and to the sub-agent's delta brief.
- The second version of the browser prototype went to the same private link. An Opus sub-agent built it from the two briefs. Before publishing, the orchestrator ran the headless test (twelve in-game days, every modifier, a three-link chain, the bare hook, the seabed, the day-end shipping) and looked at frames of every screen. The orchestrator fixed three things itself: hunters from locked depth zones no longer swim up into open water, a label no longer hides under the rod strip, and the camera returns to the surface when a new day is prepared. Not checked: real-time feel, sound, phones, balance. Nothing from the prototype goes into the Unreal game.
- The director asked for the prototype's on-screen text in Russian, his own language. The Unreal game stays English, as the jam requires. An Opus sub-agent translated every string the canvas draws and turned the headless test around: it now fails on any English word on the canvas (`Docs/briefs/prejam-prototype-v2-russian.md`).
- The director played the second prototype and called it practically ready. He approved carrying the design over to Unreal once the jam starts. His weak points: the game is too easy (about $20,000 in hand against a daily fee of $194); content should open step by step; he wants a fish info card under the cursor before the day starts; every caught fish should fly to the bucket, and the bucket should wobble, splash and sound; and one bug, a hook that could not be dragged up from the seabed. The orchestrator found why it was easy (the fee grew by 1.5 a day while income grew by about 2.3 a day as depth zones opened, depth was cheap, and nothing was left to buy after day four), compared three fixes in a critique pass, chose a steeper fee table kept as data, wrote the third edition of the content list and a brief for a third prototype (`Docs/briefs/prejam-prototype-v3.md`).
- Still before the start, the orchestrator drafted the two briefs for day one: the rules core (`Docs/briefs/day1-core.md`) and the target picture of the sea (`Docs/briefs/day1-scene.md`). They are task descriptions only, with no code and no assets. Executors receive them after 06:00.
- The third version of the browser prototype went to the same private link, with all on-screen text in Russian. An Opus sub-agent built it from `Docs/briefs/prejam-prototype-v3.md`: the hook can be dragged up from the seabed, every caught fish flies to a bucket that wobbles and splashes, a fish info card appears under the cursor, the fee has three levels to compare, and the shop opens step by step. The orchestrator ran the headless test and looked at frames of each new feature. A test bot loses around day 10-14 on the normal fee and around day 6-8 on the hard fee; a human plays better than the bot, so the director will pick the level by playing. The orchestrator fixed one thing itself: the fish card listed baits the player could not get yet. Not checked: real-time feel, sound, phones. Nothing from the prototype goes into the Unreal game.
- The director asked whether work on the game may begin before 06:00. The orchestrator checked the organisers' email: the jam starts on 12 November at 12:00 AM EST, which is 06:00 CET. Game work waits until then.

### Repository and scripts
- Empty git repository with `.gitignore` and Git LFS rules (`.gitattributes`).
- `Tools/build-editor.ps1` — compiles the editor target of a project.
- `Tools/package-win64.ps1` — packages a Development build for Windows. It has a mode for packaging while the editor stays open.
- `Tools/check_all.py` — a fast gate that every agent runs before handing work over. It checks the agent contract copy, the logs, the decision files, English-only text in the game, a clean git index and the size of the submission archive.
- `Tools/make_zip.py` — builds the submission archive from a packaged build, without debug symbols, and checks the 1 GB limit.
- `Tools/ue_mcp.py` — a small command-line client for the Unreal editor's MCP server. Any agent with a shell can inspect and change the open editor through it and can capture what the viewport shows. It repeats a call while the editor is busy and can run a Python file inside the editor.

### Agent setup
- `CLAUDE.md` and its copy `AGENTS.md` — the contract every agent reads first: jam rules, the director's decisions, who does what, how to build and check. Written in Russian, the director's language.
- `Docs/decisions/` — the director's decisions, quoted word for word. Agents may not invent game rules; every rule must point at a recorded decision.
- `Docs/briefs/` — task briefs for executor agents. These are our prompts.
- `Docs/journal.md` — the internal working journal (Russian).
- Roles: one orchestrator agent talks to the director, splits the work, writes briefs and checks every result itself. Executor agents get one brief each. By the director's rule, executors are top-tier models only.

### Tool check on a throwaway project
An empty C++ project outside the repository (`EpicJam_ToolTest`) was used to prove the pipeline before the clock starts. It contains no game content and will not be used for the game.
- The editor target compiles from the command line in about one minute.
- The editor starts its MCP server on launch. An agent listed the toolsets, loaded a level and captured the viewport as an image.
- A Development build for Windows packages in about four minutes, also while the editor is open, and the packaged executable starts.
- Finding: the agent-link plugins must be limited to the editor target in the project file, otherwise they end up inside the packaged game.
- Finding: an empty packaged build is about 1 GB on disk, of which about 380 MB is the debug symbol file. Without it the zip is 368 MB, so the game content has roughly 630 MB of room under the 1 GB limit.

### Editor capability check (still on the throwaway project)
An Opus agent mapped what an agent can really do through the editor link: levels and actors, materials, play mode and input, the C++ loop with the editor open, automated tests, interface widgets, particles, asset import. It used neutral test objects only (cubes, spheres, a test material, a "Hello" widget). The brief is in `Docs/briefs/`. The result is `Docs/research/ue59-editor-link-capabilities.md`: a verdict table and working calls that jam tasks will copy from.
- Everything in the brief works. The check took 27 minutes.
- The orchestrator verified the result itself: it captured the test level and saw the test objects and 400 coloured instances, checked that play mode was stopped, and checked that nothing else in the repository changed.
- Finding: an agent can playtest by itself. It starts play mode, presses keys, clicks interface buttons, captures frames and records video.
- Finding: an editor restart with a command-line build costs about one minute, so restarts are cheap.
- Finding: editor captures do not advance time. Anything animated must be checked in a play-mode capture or recording.
- Finding: the editor refuses calls while it runs a slow task. The client script now repeats such calls.
